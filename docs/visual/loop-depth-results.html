Looping buys depth, not arithmetic

Tiny models trained from scratch to do arithmetic in their heads. Looping the same layers lets a small model handle more steps, as well as a model 4× its size. Big numbers are a separate problem: looping doesn't fix them, and extra weights only partly do.

Read it as: "the looped model gets more than half of 2-digit-answer problems right up to 6 steps deep." Trained on depths 1–8, so depth 12 is beyond anything it practised. 1-digit inputs, normal answer order.

Does shallow work?Yes on short problems (95% at depth 3 with 1-digit answers), and it falls off fast: 47% at depth 8.
Does the loop help?Yes, a lot, for many-step problems: same weights as shallow, +16 to +29 points at depths 4–10 on small answers. Almost no help on big answers (+3 points).
Is deep better, and does it use more memory?Only on big numbers (3-digit answers at depths 3–6: +8 to +30 points over looped). On many-step problems it ties looped. It needs 4.4× the weights (memory to store), but the same compute and speed as looped.
What falls off?Everything falls with depth, and answer size matters more: 1-digit answers last to depth 12 (looped), 2-digit to 6, 3-digit to 2–3, 4-digit never pass 50% even at depth 3.
What happened with backwards answers?Writing 258 as 852 made every model worse: big answers −0.04 to −0.08, small answers −0.04 to −0.11. We keep normal order. It may only help once digits get place-value tags (next step).

Finding 1 · step count

More steps hurt every model; looping cuts the damage

Holding answer size fixed, accuracy falls as depth grows. For 1-digit answers, shallow loses 0.53 between depth 1 and 8; looped loses 0.26. The looped model tracks the deep one almost exactly, including at depths it never trained on.

Finding 3 · where each fix helps

Loops help with steps, weights help with numbers

Looping's gains sit in the left columns (small answers) and grow with depth. Extra weights add little there but help on 3-digit answers at depths 3–6 (+0.08 to +0.30), where the arithmetic itself is hard.

Finding 4 · digit order (D-007)

Writing answers backwards hurt every model

Decision rule, written before the runs: adopt reversed if big-answer accuracy rises by at least 0.05 without costing small answers more than 0.03. It failed both conditions for all three models.

This goes against published results on simple addition, where reversed digits line up with the inputs. Reversed order may only pay off together with place-value embeddings (the D-011 question).

Finding 5 · multi-digit inputs

Big inputs are hard even in one step

Depth 1 with 3-digit numbers (like 639*53) is one operation, yet no model gets above about 0.38 on any answer size. That is pure arithmetic capacity, separate from step count.

Finding 6 · test-time loops

A model trained at 8 loops only works at 8 loops

Running the looped model with fewer or more passes than it trained with breaks it. Extra loops at test time need random-loop training (plan step 5).

Small answers (1–2 digits), depths 4 and 8. The model was trained with exactly 8 passes.

What this does and doesn't show

  • Shows: with fixed weights, looping adds real sequential depth. At equal compute it matches a model with 4× the weights on many-step problems.
  • Shows: the remaining failures are mostly multi-digit arithmetic, which loops don't fix and extra weights only partly fix.
  • Doesn't show yet: that it holds across seeds (one seed so far), that extra loops at test time help (needs random-loop training), or anything beyond arithmetic (chain task and algebra later).
  • Caveat: the balanced data costs a little on easy cases (depth 3, 2-digit answers: 0.88 vs 0.94 on the earlier grid).
