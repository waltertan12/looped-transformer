Protein P0 · ESM-2 · no training

A protein model that only read letters sorted the amino acids by chemistry

ESM-2 never saw a molecule. It read millions of protein sequences as strings of 20 letters. Yet the way it stores each letter puts greasy side chains together, acids together and bases together.

What

Each amino acid is stored as a list of numbers (a point). We check whether chemically similar amino acids sit close together.

How

Take the 20 points from ESM-2. Squash them to 2D (PCA) to plot. Score in the full space: is each one's nearest neighbour in its own side-chain group?

Why

If letters alone produce chemistry, the model learned something real from sequence statistics, with no chemistry given.

So what

It shows what "an embedding learned something" means, on a result you can check with your own chemistry.

The map

The main direction is greasy versus water-loving

Each point's position along the map's main direction (PC1) against its Kyte–Doolittle hydropathy, the textbook scale from greasy (+4.5, isoleucine) to charged (−4.5, arginine). The model was never given this scale.

Letting the computer find the groups

Here no labels are given. A clustering method splits the 20 amino acids into 6 groups using only the model's numbers. Then we score how well its groups match the chemistry groups with the adjusted Rand index (ARI): 0 means no better than random, 1 means an exact match.

Chips are coloured by the chemistry group from the legend above, so a one-colour row is a clean match. Chance comes from scoring the same clusters against shuffled labels 2,000 times.

Each amino acid's nearest neighbour

Closest point in the full space (cosine similarity), for the model picked above. Dimmed rows land outside their group.

What this does and doesn't show

  • Shows: the model's built-in letter table already encodes side-chain chemistry: charge, size, aromatic rings, and greasiness.
  • Pairs a chemist would make: asparagine sits by aspartate and glutamine by glutamate. Each amide is the same shape as its acid.
  • Clustering without labels (k-means, 8M) finds: small side chains (A, G, P, C), greasy (V, L, I, M), aromatic (F, W, Y), the two alcohols (S, T), acids with asparagine (N, D, E), and bases with glutamine (Q, K, R, H). That differs from our textbook groups mainly by splitting the polar group and pairing each amide with charged neighbours.
  • Doesn't show anything about folding or structure. These are the 20 letters on their own, before the model reads any protein. Structure is the next test (P1).
  • Small numbers: only 20 points, and the 2D plot keeps about 30% of the spread. The scores use all the dimensions, and chance is measured by shuffling the group labels 2,000 times.
  • Groups are a simplification: histidine (basic, but weakly), cysteine, glycine and proline don't fit neatly anywhere.

Data: scripts/protein_p0.py, models facebook/esm2_t6_8M_UR50D and facebook/esm2_t12_35M_UR50D, run on 2026-11-24 on the CPU (6 threads, under 10 seconds). Clustering: scikit-learn k-means (200 restarts) and average-linkage hierarchical clustering, both on cosine-normalized vectors.
